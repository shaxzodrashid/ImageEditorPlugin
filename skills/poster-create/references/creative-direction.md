# Creative direction

Use this reference when the brief defines the message but not a complete composition. The goal is a visual thesis: one memorable relationship between message, subject, and brand—not a decorated layout.

## Read the brand visually

Inspect both written rules and rendered examples. Extract four kinds of evidence:

| Evidence | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Mandates | Required canvas, background, logo, type, colors, safe zones, and locked structures |
| Expressive constants | Recurring crop behavior, subject scale, whitespace, shape language, contrast, energy, and tone |
| Open choices | Media, visual device, palette emphasis, composition, and pacing the brand permits |
| Production assets | Supplied templates, logos, shapes, grids, fonts, product images, and missing files |

Treat rules as boundaries and examples as evidence of character. Use a locked template when the system requires it. If only examples are available, derive the visual grammar without copying one example as the universal layout.

## Avoid creative lock-in

When recent approved work is accessible, inspect the latest three to five relevant posters or campaigns. Fingerprint each by concept premise, hero treatment, scale/crop, dominant axis, copy relationship, visual device, and palette emphasis.

Do not ban every repeated brand element; that destroys recognition. Instead, avoid repeating the same combination without a deliberate campaign reason. A familiar logo zone or type system can remain invariant while the premise, subject relationship, crop logic, or visual device changes.

If no recent work is available, record that limitation and rely on the divergence and thumbnail stages rather than choosing the first familiar poster formula.

## Build the creative brief

Write these before ideating:

- **Audience tension:** What question, desire, friction, or misconception creates attention?
- **Benefit:** What becomes easier, better, safer, faster, or more desirable?
- **Proof:** What product detail, mechanism, result, contrast, or human behavior makes the claim believable?
- **Emotion:** What should the viewer feel in the first second?
- **Visual job:** What must the visual communicate before the supporting copy is read?

If the visual job only repeats the headline literally, sharpen it.

## Diverge before selecting

Generate four to six directions. Use these as prompts for range, not as fixed templates:

- make an exact product detail become the composition;
- show cause and effect, comparison, or transformation;
- turn a product feature into a spatial or material metaphor;
- make typography and subject physically interact;
- use human gesture or use context as the proof;
- reveal a mechanism through a diagram, layers, or sequence;
- create tension through extreme scale, crop, interruption, or negative space;
- let a brand shape frame, hide, reveal, or connect the subject.

Each direction must specify:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Concept sentence | Explain the idea in one plain sentence without style adjectives |
| Message link | State why this visual proves or sharpens this exact message |
| Hero and evidence | Name the focal subject and the detail that makes it credible |
| Spatial premise | Describe scale, crop, alignment, overlap, direction, and negative space |
| Brand expression | Name the permitted brand devices that make it ownable |
| Medium | Photography, collage, illustration, 3D, typography, diagram, or a justified hybrid |
| Feasibility | Source/generation plan, exact-product risk, and technical difficulty |

Fail divergence when two directions retain the same premise and spatial skeleton with only a new color, crop, background, or art style.

## Reject weak directions early

Reject a direction when any of these are true:

- it is described mainly by adjectives such as “premium,” “cinematic,” or “futuristic”;
- the hero is an attractive but interchangeable stock or generated image;
- the visual is merely adjacent to the topic rather than evidence for the message;
- the copy and visual occupy separate zones without a meaningful relationship;
- random novelty replaces product truth or campaign coherence;
- it needs many decorative devices to feel interesting;
- it violates a hard brand rule or depends on an unavailable locked asset;
- it cannot survive at mobile thumbnail size.

For a camera-quality topic, for example, a generic dark lens close-up is atmosphere. Exact-product macro, sensor-to-result comparison, light becoming processed pixels, or a real capture moment are different visual premises. Do not reuse these examples mechanically; derive premise options from the actual message.

## Select without averaging away the idea

Score each direction from 0-3 on:

- message specificity;
- first-second stopping power;
- brand ownability;
- one-focal-point clarity;
- emotional fit;
- product/source truth;
- feasibility at delivery quality;
- distinctness from recent work and neighboring carousel slides.

Disqualify any direction with `0` for message specificity, brand compliance, product truth, or feasibility. Prefer one decisive idea over a compromise that combines every direction.

Record why the selected direction won and why each alternative lost. This prevents the first plausible idea from winning by default.

Compare the selected direction with the recent-work fingerprints. If the same premise, hero treatment, and spatial skeleton recur together, select or develop another direction unless continuity is intentional and documented.

## Thumbnail the composition

Create low-fidelity thumbnails for the strongest three directions at the exact output ratio. Use simple blocks, the real headline length, and approximate subject silhouette; do not spend time on polished generation yet.

Compare at full size and approximately 25% size:

- Is the focal subject obvious before reading?
- Does the reading path have a deliberate first, second, and third beat?
- Is the headline integrated with the visual logic?
- Does negative space look intentional?
- Do the three thumbnails remain different when blurred or reduced to silhouettes?

If all three reduce to “logo + image + headline,” return to divergence.

## Control carousel coherence and variation

Define two to four **invariants** across the set, such as logo zone, type system, palette logic, one shape language, one material/lighting world, or one transition motif.

Then assign **variables** per slide, such as hero scale, camera angle, crop direction, copy-to-subject relationship, visual device, dominant axis, density, or negative-space location.

Use repetition as a continuity device only when intentional. The same exact product or campaign hero may recur when it improves recognition and truth, but its role or relationship must advance the story. Avoid the same static crop, placement, and copy block on consecutive slides. Do not force unrelated imagery merely to make every slide different.

Plan rhythm: hook, explanation, contrast or proof, payoff, action. Let information density rise and fall rather than making every slide equally loud.

## Run anti-generic tests

Apply these before polishing and again to the final export:

- **Swap test:** If another electronics brand or unrelated product could replace the logo/hero without changing the layout, the concept is not ownable enough.
- **Why-this-visual test:** Name the exact message detail the visual communicates. “It looks good” fails.
- **Relationship test:** Explain how copy and subject interact through scale, crop, overlap, direction, contrast, or sequence.
- **One-second test:** At a glance, the viewer should perceive the subject, emotional tone, and primary question/benefit.
- **Thumbnail test:** The focal hierarchy must survive at feed size without relying on small copy.
- **Premise test:** Describe the poster as an idea, not a list of layers.
- **Subtraction test:** Remove one decorative device. If the concept disappears, the device was doing decoration's job instead of the idea's job.

Failure means revise the premise, hero, or composition. Do not try to rescue a generic concept with more glow, gradients, glass cards, badges, or effects.

## Iterate at the right level

Diagnose a weak preview in this order:

1. wrong or generic premise;
2. weak subject-message relationship;
3. timid scale or crop;
4. unclear hierarchy or negative space;
5. off-brand visual behavior;
6. execution defects.

Fix the highest failing level first. Polishing masks, shadows, and kerning cannot repair a missing visual idea.
